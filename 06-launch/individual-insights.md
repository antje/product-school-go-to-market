# Launch Plan & Individual Insights: Roamly Groups

> Module 6 · Launch · Deliverable 6
>
> How Roamly Groups goes to market: a city-by-city closed launch that releases before it launches, enables the host side before the traveler side, starts on owned channels, and pulls back on numbers set in advance. Then what I learned building it.

## 1. Launch plan

**What kind of launch.** A closed launch, city by city, not a big bang. Two reasons. The product only works where at least 60% of vetted hosts hold seats for groups, so it cannot go everywhere at once. And if the promise ("the group books together or nobody is charged") fails in public, it is expensive to walk back.

**Release and launch are separate decisions.** The group checkout is released first, quietly, to the opted-in hosts of one city, with no campaign. It is launched in that city only after one quarter of real group bookings shows that hosts keep holding seats and groups complete. The full quarter runs once, in the first city; a quarter in each of 40 cities in turn would take ten years. Later cities still release before they launch, but on the 60% opt-in gate and a four to six week release window, several cities at a time.

| Phase | Goal | Key activities | Owner |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Pre-launch** (release and early access: one quarter in the first city, then four to six weeks per city) | Prove that hosts keep holding seats and that groups complete, in one city, before anyone outside it hears about the product | Host partnerships signs vetted hosts onto Group-ready on the real hold terms; the city goes ahead only at 60% opt-in. Release to those hosts' listings. Invite the organizers who filed split-payment tickets, by email, as early access. Track host retention, holds released early, group completion and per-friend completion for the quarter. | PM, with host partnerships (sign-up) and support (first tickets) |
| **Launch** (per city, once the pilot passes) | A coordinated moment that turns organizers already on Roamly into group bookers | Turn on the "booking for a group?" notification at checkout for segment organizers. Launch email to repeat users in the city. Landing page. The social asset seeded with trip-planning creators and travel communities in the city. "Booked as a group of 6" on share and review surfaces. Launch on a Tuesday or Wednesday, clear of public holidays, before the city's peak group-travel season, timed from that city's typical booking lead time, so organizers see it while they plan. | PM and marketing, with growth for the in-product surfaces |
| **Post-launch** (from week 5 in each city) | Sustain and compound: more seats per booking, more hosts, more cities | Sell Group Plus to hosts who have seen group bookings. Put Groups into lifecycle emails (post-trip, before the next booking). Collect organizer stories for the landing page. Open the next city when it passes the 60% gate. Paid channels only after the completion bar holds for a quarter. | PM, host partnerships (Plus), marketing (stories, lifecycle) |

### Enablement: informing is not enabling

The motion is a hybrid. Travelers arrive self-serve, which is product-led. Hosts are signed one by one by host partnerships, which is sales-led. So both enablement sequences run at once, each at its own pace.

1. **Leadership first:** the pricing (host commission, three tiers, no traveler fee), the 60% city gate, and the tripwires below are agreed before anyone else is briefed.
2. **Host side, sales-led order:** host partnerships, then host success, then support. Hosts must sign before a city can launch, so the supply team is enabled first.
3. **Traveler side, product-led order:** support is ready before the first friend opens a link, because self-serve travelers arrive with questions on day one.
4. **Marketing last:** positioning is locked (Module 3) before any external copy is written.

| Team | What they need to do their job without me | The asset |
|---|---|---|
| Leadership | The bet, the revenue model, the metrics and the tripwires | This page and the Module 5 memo |
| Host partnerships (the sales team for supply) | How to win a host against Viator, the hold objection and its answer, the Group Plus math | The Viator battlecard; a host one-pager with the sales line ("full groups that have already paid; seats back by the deadline") and "Plus pays for itself at one extra seat every 3.8 groups" |
| Host success (the CS team for hosts) | Exactly what a host was promised: hold length, release deadline, commission per tier, what each tier includes | Tier breakdown from Module 5; the hold terms in writing |
| Support | Answers to the first 48 hours of traveler questions, and when to escalate | FAQ: What does "held" mean? When am I charged? What if a friend never pays? What if the group does not fill? Can I get a refund? Escalate: any charge to a traveler whose group did not fill, and any host releasing a held group before the deadline. |
| Marketing | The positioning statement, the two pillars, the words to use | Module 3 and Module 4; the word rules below |

**The word rules, settled here.** Two open flags from the Module 4 cold reads close with this launch. What a friend pays for is a **seat**. What the group books is an **experience**. In customer copy the person running it is the **host**. And every surface names Roamly as "the app for hand-picked local experiences" once, because cold readers could not tell what Roamly was.

**Enablement checklist, answered before each city launches:** success metrics and the 30-day monitoring plan are agreed (leadership); host partnerships can explain the hold and answer the top three host objections without the one-pager (the hold, the commission, "why not just Viator"); host success can state each tier's terms; support has the FAQ and the escalation path; every external asset has been checked against the messaging pillars and the word rules.

### Channels: owned first, paid last

- **Owned, the foundation:** the in-app checkout notification, the launch email, the split-payment ticket cohort, share and review surfaces. The organizer is already a Roamly user, so owned channels reach the exact audience at no cost.
- **Earned, built into the product:** every group booking sends a link to up to seven people who are mostly not on Roamly. Creator seeding in each launch city adds to that. That makes the invite link the main earned channel.
- **Paid, not at launch:** paid reach would bring organizers who are not yet Roamly users, which is outside the Module 1 segment, and it is the hardest channel to attribute. It opens only after the completion bar holds for a quarter in a city.

## 2. Launch metrics & guardrails

Each metric points back to a decision made earlier, so a bad number tells me which decision to revisit.

| Signal | Bar | If it misses, the decision to revisit |
|---|---|---|
| Host opt-in per city (acquisition, supply side) | At least 60% of vetted hosts before launch (Module 2: at 60% a three-option shortlist has a group-bookable choice 94% of the time) | The hold terms and the host pitch |
| Group completion rate (activation) | At least 80% of the individual booking completion rate (Module 1) | The friend flow and its message |
| Seats per group booking against a holdout (the expand outcome) | At least 4 (Module 1) | The segment |
| Group Plus uptake among hosts who have seen group bookings (conversion) | Plus hosts fill at least one extra seat every 3.8 groups (Module 5) | The Plus price and contents |
| Support tickets about unexpected charges (enablement) | None tolerated: each one breaks the core promise | The support brief and the payment flow |

- **Success looks like:** in the first launch city, after one quarter, at least 60% of the hosts who opted in are still opted in, groups complete at 80% or more of the individual rate, and each group booking carries four or more seats against the holdout.
- **Tripwire (when to pull back):** pause a city if host retention falls below 60% after a quarter (the gate it launched on), if held slots released unpaid exceed the cost the hosts agreed to, or if hosts release more than 5% of held groups before the deadline. Redesign the friend flow before widening if per-friend completion is below 90% (Module 1). Pause Group Plus in a city if it pulls Group-ready opt-in below 60% (Module 5). Stop immediately on any traveler charged for a group that did not fill.
- **Who decides:** the PM and the host partnerships lead, at a monthly review of the dashboard, with leadership informed the same week. A stop on a wrongful charge needs no review: engineering pauses the flow on the alert.
- **A miss triggers a look before a verdict.** Low group completion can mean the friend flow is broken or that the wrong experiences are being booked as groups. The per-friend drop point tells them apart: friends who open the link and leave at payment point to the flow; groups where friends never open the link point to the choice of experience or its price. Low host retention can mean the hold is too costly or that group demand never came; holds released unpaid against groups completed tell those apart.
- **The hold itself, as a hypothesis to validate in the pilot:** 48 hours from the first invite, closing no later than 72 hours before the experience. Shorter than the 72-hour hold Airbnb's hosts rejected, long enough for friends in different time zones to pay, and it gives the host three days to resell released seats. The 5% early-release bar is a judgment: above it, roughly one group in twenty sees the promise break.

## 3. The full GTM story in one slide

Roamly already has group demand and loses it: organizers try to book for friends, front the money, and the sessions die (Module 1). The competitor that matters is Viator, which holds seats for free but still charges one card. The gap no one fills is confirming a whole group at once, which only works if the host agrees to hold the seats. Airbnb tried that in 2017 and retired it in 2018 because its hosts never opted in. So the bet is to own the moment a group has chosen and one person is about to become the bank, and to win the hosts first (Module 2). The positioning names that promise: each friend pays their own seat while the host holds the spots, and the group books together or nobody is charged (Module 3). The messaging splits it for the organizer, who never has to be the bank, and the friend, who pays in one tap (Module 4). The pricing follows: the host gains the filled capacity, so the host pays through the commission, and travelers never see a fee (Module 5). The launch goes city by city, only where hosts hold, with the host side enabled first (Module 6). The through-line: the hold is the product, and the host decides whether it exists, so every decision starts with the host.

## 4. Individual insights

- **Biggest "aha" across the certification:** The product is a supply policy, not a checkout screen. I started in Module 1 thinking about the organizer's pain. Competitive research showed that Airbnb built this exact feature in 2017 and killed it because hosts hated holding seats. From then on, the hold became the bet, the core product truth, the thing the asset had to show, and the reason the host pays in the pricing.
- **What changed in how you think about GTM:** Every downstream decision is only as good as the upstream one it traces to. Pricing fell out of the bet: once the hold was the moat, the host was the obvious payer. The launch plan fell out of pricing: hosts are signed first, so their team is enabled first. And I learned to cold-read everything. Four blind reads of one social post each found a different flaw, including a statistic that contradicted the product's own mechanic.
- **What you'd do differently with more time / data:** Talk to hosts before the pricing, not after. I derived the 60% gate, the commission uplift and the break-even from structure, because the scenario has no data. With access, I would start with a dozen host interviews and the real drop-off rate on group sessions. Those two inputs replace most of my assumptions.
- **The one idea you'll apply at work on Monday:** Before any launch I own, ask each team "can you do your job without me?" instead of "did I share everything?", and write one brief per team that answers it.

**Friction, and how I worked through it.** Numbers I could not source. The scenario gives no data, and every figure I wanted either came from AI output I had to check or had to be derived from the structure. Two AI-drafted claims turned out wrong on inspection: a 38% statistic cited to the wrong page, and a price threshold nobody could source. I recomputed every number and verified every citation before it went into a deliverable, and I labeled the one invented figure as illustrative.
