# Roamly Groups · GTM Strategy, Module 1

## Discover Framework

### 1 · Align on the Goal

**Business problem**

The core business problem is that Roamly already has group demand and is losing it. People who want to book together split across separate accounts, pay each other back by hand, and drop out before the booking completes. Each failed group session is four or six lost customers, not one. The product only knows how to sell one seat to one person, so a booking unit customers are asking for goes unserved. The job is to convert the group demand we can already see into completed bookings, without breaking the individual experience that earns 3x industry repeat rates.

**Success looks like**

If this launch goes well, a group booking completes at close to the rate an individual booking does today, and drop-off on multi-person sessions stops being a support topic. Value per booking rises because one booking carries several seats, and hosts fill sessions that used to run half empty. Every group brings non-users onto Roamly through the organizer's invites. The brand becomes the way you book experiences together, not just where solo travelers find good tours. And the organizer, usually already a loyal user, stops doing unpaid coordination work and fronting money for friends.

### 2 · Do the Research

**Signals of opportunity**

The signals that suggest a real opportunity are: support has flagged the pattern, so it is frequent enough to show up in tickets; users are already coordinating across accounts and settling payments manually, so intent is strong enough that people fight the product to do it; drop-off on those sessions is high, so the failure is in coordination and payment, not in desire; and the feedback is consistent, so it is not one loud segment. What the data does not tell us yet: how many sessions carry group signals, typical party size, which cities, and whether the organizer is an existing repeat user. Those four numbers are the first research request.

**Candidate segments**

1) Friend groups on a shared trip, 3 to 8 people, one person organizing.
2) Families and multi-generational trips, one adult booking for the whole party.
3) Celebration travel: bachelor and bachelorette weekends, milestone birthdays, reunions; high urgency, high willingness to pay.
4) Small company teams: offsites and team days, booked by a team lead on one company card.
5) Community and creator-led trips: travel creators, alumni groups, clubs, study-abroad cohorts, where the organizer books for people they may not know well.

### 3 · Build a Working Team

**Stakeholders**

Support (for the ticket evidence and the language customers use), Analytics (for sizing the drop-off and the party-size distribution), Finance (for take rate on a multi-seat booking and the cash flow of split payments), Payments and risk (for split-pay mechanics, refunds when one person cancels, chargebacks, fraud), Host operations (for whether hosts can take groups, capacity per experience, and group pricing options), Marketing and growth (for the channels that reach organizers and how the invite flow becomes an acquisition loop), Legal and compliance (for payment regulation on money moving between users), Customer research (for interviews with organizers who hit the drop-off). Sales or partnerships only if we pursue company teams, which is a different motion.

### 4 · Form Your Hypothesis

**Segment to target first**

I'd target friend groups with one organizer, 3 to 8 people, where the organizer is an existing Roamly user, first because it is the demand already in the support data: existing users are hacking it today, so we are removing friction from a booking that almost happened, not creating new demand. It fits how Roamly already grows: the organizer is a repeat user who trusts the product, and the invite step pulls their friends in as new users, with no new supply and no new channel. Every booking carries several seats, so revenue per booking rises without touching the individual product. Celebration travel sits inside this segment as the highest-urgency case. Company teams have higher order values but need invoicing, a buyer who is not the user, and a human-led motion, so they are a second phase.

**Core value proposition**

For friend groups and the person who organizes them, Roamly Groups lets you book experiences for your whole group in one place. Everyone sees the plan, everyone pays their own share, and nobody has to chase anyone. For the organizer: no spreadsheet, no fronting the money, no reminding friends to pay you back.

## GTM Motion

**Primary motion**

PLG

**Justification**

I chose this motion because, across the five inputs, everything lands on PLG. Time to value is minutes: the organizer creates the group, shares a link, friends pick and pay. Buyer and user are the same person. Contract value is low, one booking of tens to a few hundred dollars per seat, with no procurement. The cycle is self-serve; nobody at Roamly should touch a group booking. It scales through product virality, because every group booking sends invites to people who are not on Roamly yet. Community is a launch channel, not a second motion: Roamly already grows through travel communities and social media, so we seed the feature where organizers already talk to each other instead of buying reach. Not SLG: company teams would need invoicing and a human-led cycle, which is a later phase.

## Strategic Inputs

**Primary goal**

Expand

**Target audience**

My target audience is active Roamly users with 2+ bookings in the last 12 months who show group signals (multiple accounts on the same experience and time slot, split-payment support tickets, "book for my friends" feedback), in the cities where those signals cluster. They are the organizers we reach. The 2 to 7 friends they invite, mostly not on Roamly today, are who we have to convert.

## Pressure-test

**Assumption I'm challenging**

I want to push back on the claim that "multiple accounts on the same slot" means seats Roamly already has, so Groups adds zero revenue, because the evidence says the opposite. Drop-off on these group sessions is high, so those are bookings that started and died: seats we are losing, not seats we own. And Roamly's current users are predominantly independent travelers, so most of the friends an organizer invites are not on Roamly today. Expand stands as the goal. I accepted the rest: the friend is the majority of the transaction and my value proposition said nothing to them, so the refined strategy adds a friend-side value line, guest pay, a slot hold while the group pays, and group completion rate as the leading indicator.

## V1 GTM Strategy One-Pager

**Positioning**

For people who organize trips for their friends and end up fronting the money and chasing everyone to pay them back, Roamly Groups is the group checkout that holds the slot while every friend pays their own share. Unlike a group chat plus a payment app, the booking confirms for everyone at once and nobody is out of pocket.

**Key messages**

1. To the organizer: Never front the money again. Pick the experience, invite the group, and each friend pays their own seat. No spreadsheet, no reminders, no IOUs.
2. To the friend: One tap and your seat is yours. Pay your share from the link. No app to install, no account to create before you pay, nobody chasing you.
3. To both: Confirmed together or charged never. Roamly holds the slot while the group pays. If the group does not complete, nobody is charged and nobody is stuck.

**Channel approach**

Primary, in product: a "booking for a group?" entry point at checkout for segment organizers in launch cities, a prompt after a booking that matches the same-slot pattern, and a direct email to the users who filed split-pay tickets. The loop, invite link: SMS or shared link to the friends, guest pay in one screen, account offered after payment; this step decides the completion rate and gets the design budget. Seeding, launch cities only: trip-planning creators and travel communities, plus "booked as a group of 6" on Roamly's share and review surfaces. Not at launch: paid acquisition, company teams, itinerary planning as the headline.

**Success metric**

Group completion rate: the share of started group bookings that reach fully paid confirmation. Baseline is the current drop-off on same-slot group sessions, and the target is a lift over that number once measured. It proves the PLG loop works because it is where the friend-side flow succeeds or fails, and it moves weeks before seats per booking does. Seats per booking among segment organizers, read against a holdout, is the success signal behind it.
