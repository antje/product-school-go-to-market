# Roamly Groups · GTM Strategy, Module 1 (Exercise 1)

## Responses

### Step 1 · Align on the Goal

**What is the core business problem Roamly is trying to solve with Roamly Groups?**

Roamly already has group demand and is losing it. People who want to book together split across separate accounts, pay each other back by hand, and drop out before the booking completes. Each failed group session is not one lost customer but four or six. The problem is not awareness and not price. The product only knows how to sell to one person at a time, so a whole booking unit customers are asking for goes unserved. The job is to convert the group demand we can already see into completed bookings, and to open a larger booking unit without breaking the individual experience that earns 3x industry repeat rates.

**What does success look like if this launch goes well? Think beyond revenue, the product, the brand, and the customer relationship.**

- Product: a group booking completes at close to the rate an individual booking does today. Drop-off on multi-person sessions stops being a support topic.
- Revenue: value per booking goes up because one booking now carries several seats, and hosts fill sessions that used to run half empty.
- Growth: every group brings non-users onto Roamly. The organizer invites the friends, the friends create accounts to pay their share. That is an acquisition loop we do not have today.
- Brand: Roamly becomes the way you book experiences together, not just the place solo travelers find good tours.
- Customer relationship: the organizer, usually already a loyal user, stops doing unpaid coordination work for us and stops fronting money for their friends.

### Step 2 · Do the Research

**What signals suggest this is a real market opportunity worth pursuing? What is the behavioral data telling you about unmet demand?**

Support has flagged the pattern, so it is frequent enough to show up in tickets. Users are already coordinating across accounts and settling payments manually, which means intent is strong: people are fighting the product to do this. Drop-off on those sessions is high, so the failure is in coordination and payment, not in desire. The feedback is consistent, so it is not one loud segment.

What the data does not tell us yet: how many sessions carry group signals, typical party size, which cities, and whether the organizer is an existing repeat user. Those four numbers are the first research request.

**List your top 5 possible segments that could benefit from Roamly Groups.**

1. Friend groups on a shared trip, 3 to 8 people, one person organizing.
2. Families and multi-generational trips, one adult booking for the whole party.
3. Celebration travel: bachelor and bachelorette weekends, milestone birthdays, reunions. High urgency, high willingness to pay.
4. Small company teams: offsites and team days, booked by a team lead or office manager on one company card.
5. Community and creator-led trips: travel creators, alumni groups, clubs, study-abroad cohorts, where the organizer books for people they may not know well.

**How might you size the market? Think industry reports, travel research, social listening, competitor analysis.**

- Top-down: share of leisure trips taken with friends or family from industry travel reports, times tours-and-activities spend per trip, narrowed to Roamly's 40 cities.
- Bottom-up from our own data: sessions with group signals (multiple accounts on the same experience and time slot, support tickets, split-payment mentions) times average party size times average experience price, times the completion uplift we expect.
- Social listening: volume of "group trip planning" and "who owes what" complaints on Reddit, TikTok and travel forums.
- Competitor analysis: what Airbnb Experiences, GetYourGuide, Viator and Klook do for groups today, and what people bolt on (group chats, spreadsheets, Splitwise) when the platforms do nothing.

### Step 3 · Build a Working Team

**Which cross-functional stakeholders would you include to validate and execute? Think beyond product and engineering, who has context on the customer, market, and commercial model?**

- Support: owns the ticket evidence and the language customers use.
- Data and analytics: sizes the drop-off and the party-size distribution.
- Finance: models take rate on a multi-seat booking and the cash flow of split payments.
- Payments and risk: split payment mechanics, refunds when one person cancels, chargebacks, fraud.
- Host operations and supply: can hosts take groups, max capacity per experience, whether group rates need a new host pricing option.
- Marketing and growth: which channels reach organizers, and how the invite flow becomes an acquisition loop.
- Legal and compliance: money moving between users may trigger payment regulation in some markets.
- Customer research: interviews with organizers who hit the drop-off, so we build for the real job.
- Sales or partnerships only if we pursue company teams, which is a different motion.

### Step 4 · Form Your Hypothesis

**Which one segment is the most valuable to target first, and why?**

Friend groups with one organizer, 3 to 8 people, where the organizer is an existing Roamly user.

- It is the demand already in the support data. Existing users are hacking it today, so we are removing friction from a booking that almost happened, not creating new demand.
- It fits how Roamly already grows. The organizer is a repeat user who trusts the product, and the invite step pulls their friends in as new users. No new supply, no new channel.
- Every booking carries several seats, so revenue per booking rises without touching the individual product.
- Celebration travel sits inside this segment as the highest-urgency case, so we get it for free.
- Company teams have higher order values but need invoicing, a buyer who is not the user, and a human-led motion. That is a second phase, not the first.

**What is the core value proposition for that segment?**

Book experiences for your whole group in one place. Everyone sees the plan, everyone pays their own share, and nobody has to chase anyone. For the organizer: no spreadsheet, no fronting the money, no reminding friends to pay you back.
