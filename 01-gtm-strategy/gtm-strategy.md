# GTM Strategy: Roamly Groups

> Module 1 · Build Your V1 GTM Strategy · Deliverable 1
>
> A V1 strategy for taking Roamly Groups, a coordinated group-booking feature with split payments and shared itineraries, to market. It runs the opportunity through the Discover Framework, commits to a motion and a goal, pressure-tests the inputs, and ends with a one-pager and the assumption the strategy depends on.

## 1. The Discover Framework

The four class steps (Align on the Goal, Do the Research, Build a Team, Form a Hypothesis) come first. The template's eight lenses follow, filled from them.

**Align on the goal.** Roamly already has group demand and is losing it. Success looks like this:

- Product: a group booking completes at close to the rate an individual booking does today, and drop-off on multi-person sessions stops being a support topic.
- Revenue: hosts fill sessions that used to run half empty, and each booking carries several seats.
- Growth: every group brings non-users onto Roamly through the organizer's invites.
- Brand: Roamly becomes the place to book experiences together, as well as where solo travelers find good tours.
- Customer relationship: the organizer, usually already a loyal user, stops doing unpaid coordination work and fronting money for friends.

**Do the research.** The signals are in the support queue: users coordinating across separate accounts, settling split payments by hand, syncing availability, dropping off before the booking completes, and saying consistently that they want to do this on Roamly. What the data does not tell us yet: how many sessions carry group signals, typical party size, which cities, and whether the organizer is an existing repeat user. Those four numbers are the first research request. Sizing runs four ways: top-down from the share of leisure trips taken with friends or family times tours-and-activities spend per trip, narrowed to Roamly's 40 cities; bottom-up from our own sessions with group signals times average party size times average price times the completion uplift; social listening on "group trip planning" and "who owes what" complaints on Reddit, TikTok and travel forums; and competitor analysis of what Airbnb Experiences, GetYourGuide, Viator and Klook do for groups, and what people bolt on (group chats, spreadsheets, Splitwise) when the platforms do nothing.

**Build a working team.** Support (the ticket evidence and the language customers use). Analytics (drop-off and party-size distribution). Finance (take rate on a multi-seat booking, cash flow of split payments). Payments and risk (split-pay mechanics, refunds when one person cancels, chargebacks, fraud). Host operations (can hosts take groups, capacity per experience, group pricing options). Marketing and growth (channels that reach organizers, the invite flow as an acquisition loop). Legal and compliance (payment regulation on money moving between users). Customer research (interviews with organizers who hit the drop-off). Sales or partnerships only if company teams are pursued, which is a different motion.

**Form the hypothesis.** Friend groups with one organizer, 3 to 8 people, where the organizer is an existing Roamly user, are the segment to serve first. The value proposition, launch requirements and metrics below follow from that choice.

| Lens | Answer |
|---|---|
| **D**emand: who has the problem and how strong is the pull? | People booking Roamly experiences for a group. Support already sees them coordinating across separate accounts, splitting payments by hand and syncing availability. Drop-off on those sessions is high and the feedback is consistent: they want to do this on Roamly and the product gets in the way. They want it enough to work around the product. |
| **I**deal customer profile (ICP) | The organizer: an existing Roamly user with 2+ bookings in the last 12 months who plans a trip for 3 to 8 friends. Today they open a group chat, a spreadsheet and a payment app, front the money, and chase friends to pay them back. Consumer, self-funded, no approval needed beyond the friends saying yes. |
| **S**egment and positioning angle | Five candidate segments: friend groups; families and multi-generational trips; celebration travel (bachelor and bachelorette weekends, milestone birthdays, reunions); small company teams booked by a team lead on one card; community and creator-led trips. Friend groups win because the demand is already in the support data, the organizer is a loyal user, and no new supply or channel is needed. Celebration travel sits inside it as the highest-urgency case. Company teams need invoicing, a buyer who is not the user and a human-led motion, so they are a later phase. Angle: the organizer never fronts the money and never chases anyone. |
| **C**hannels that reach them | In product: a group entry point at checkout for segment organizers, and a direct email to the users who filed split-payment tickets. The invite link is the channel to the friends. Seeding in travel communities and with trip-planning creators in launch cities. No paid acquisition. |
| **O**ffer / value proposition | Organizer: you never front the money and you never chase anyone. Friend: one tap and your seat is yours, no account needed to pay. Both: Roamly holds the slot while the group pays, so the booking confirms for everyone at once or nobody is charged. |
| **V**alidation signal (what proves demand?) | Started group sessions that today die: multiple accounts on the same experience and time slot, split-payment support tickets, and "book for my friends" feedback. Demand is proven when those sessions complete at a rate close to individual bookings once the product supports them. |
| **E**conomics (rough CAC vs. value) | Near-zero CAC: the organizer is already a user and reaches the friends for us. Value per booking rises from one seat to 3 to 8 seats at tens to a few hundred dollars each. The cost to watch is the slot hold: seats held during the payment window that release unpaid. |
| **R**isks and unknowns | The friend is the majority of the transaction and has no pain today (a payment app already works for them). Each friend is a separate conversion; at 80% per friend a group of seven completes about 21% of the time. Unknowns: the current drop-off rate on group sessions, party-size distribution, which cities the signals cluster in, and who bears the cost of a held slot that releases unpaid. |

## 2. The problem & the audience

- **Problem worth solving:** Roamly already has group demand and is losing it. The product only knows how to sell one seat to one person, so a whole booking unit that customers are asking for goes unserved. Each failed group session loses four or six customers, not one. The job is to convert the group demand we can already see into completed bookings, and to open a larger booking unit without breaking the individual experience that earns repeat rates three times the industry average.
- **Primary ICP:** The organizer described above, in the cities where failed group sessions cluster. They are who we reach. The friends they invite, mostly not on Roamly today, are who we have to convert.
- **What they do today (the alternative):** Group chat plus a payment app. The organizer books seats one by one or across several accounts, pays up front, and collects from friends afterwards. It works well enough for the friends. The organizer carries the work and the financial risk.

## 3. GTM motion

**Chosen motion:** product-led, with community seeding as a launch channel.

All five motion inputs land on PLG. Time to value is minutes: the organizer creates the group, shares a link, and friends pick and pay. Buyer and user are the same person. Contract value is low, one booking of tens to a few hundred dollars per seat, with no procurement. The cycle is self-serve; nobody at Roamly should touch a group booking. It scales through product virality, because every group booking sends invites to people who are not on Roamly yet.

Community is a channel here, not a motion. Roamly already grows through travel communities, review sites and social media, and group travel is peer-influenced, so the launch seeds the feature where organizers already talk to each other instead of buying reach. Sales-led is out: company teams would need invoicing, a buyer who is not the user and a human-led cycle, which is a different product and a later phase.

**Primary goal:** Expand. Grow within the existing base by recovering the group bookings that today start and die, so each booking carries several seats. New-user signups from invites are measured as a by-product, not set as the launch goal.

- **Acquisition:** Organizers come from inside the base through the checkout entry point and the split-pay ticket cohort. Friends come through the invite link.
- **Activation:** The group is activated when every friend has paid and the booking confirms for all of them at once. The invite screen decides activation, so it gets the design and testing budget: guest pay in one screen, account creation offered after payment.
- **Monetization:** Same take rate on every seat, now several seats per booking. No group discount at launch. The slot hold is the one new cost, and its window and cost owner are decided before launch.

## 4. V1 one-pager

**What the pressure test changed.** A skeptical review raised three challenges against the first draft of these inputs. First, that "multiple accounts on the same slot" means seats Roamly already has, so Groups adds no revenue and Expand is the wrong goal. Rejected on the evidence: drop-off on those sessions is high, so they are bookings that started and died, and current users are predominantly independent travelers, so most invited friends are not on Roamly today. Expand stands. Second, that the whole strategy was written from the organizer's chair while the friends are the majority of the transaction, each one a separate conversion with no pain today. Accepted: the value proposition gained a friend-side line, guest pay and a slot hold became launch requirements, and group completion rate became the leading indicator. Third, that itinerary planning and a community "motion" widened the story. Accepted in part: split pay leads the message and itinerary ships in the product, and community is a seeding channel, not a motion.

> For people who organize trips for their friends and end up fronting the money and chasing everyone to pay them back, Roamly Groups is a group checkout that holds the slot while every friend pays their own share. Unlike a group chat plus a payment app, the booking confirms for everyone at once and nobody is out of pocket.

**Key messages**

1. To the organizer: *Never front the money again.* Pick the experience, invite the group, and each friend pays their own seat. No spreadsheet, no reminders, no IOUs.
2. To the friend: *One tap and your seat is yours.* Pay your share from the link. No app to install, no account to create before you pay, nobody chasing you.
3. To both: *Confirmed together or charged never.* Roamly holds the slot while the group pays. If the group does not complete, nobody is charged and nobody is stuck.

**Channel approach**

| Role | Channel | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| Primary | In product | A "booking for a group?" entry point at checkout for segment organizers in launch cities. A prompt after a booking that matches the same-slot pattern. A direct email to the users who filed split-pay tickets. |
| The loop | Invite link | SMS or shared link to the friends. Guest pay in one screen, account offered after payment. This step decides the completion rate. |
| Seeding | Communities and creators | Trip-planning creators and travel communities in launch cities. "Booked as a group of 6" on Roamly's share and review surfaces, so every completed group is visible to the next organizer. |
| Not at launch | Paid acquisition, company teams, itinerary as the headline | Itinerary planning ships in the product. Split pay leads the message. |

**Launch requirements:** a slot hold for the payment window (without it the organizer fronts money or loses the slot, and the value proposition is false); guest pay for friends in one screen with account creation after payment; split pay as the headline; a city gate on the entry point so the launch runs where the group signals cluster.

**Open before launch:** the hold window length and who bears the cost when a held slot releases unpaid; what the one-screen friend flow looks like and the completion rate it has to beat; the current drop-off rate and the organizer count in the launch cities, so the target and the ceiling are written down rather than asserted.

*Refined in Module 2:* competitive research showed Airbnb shipped this hold in 2017 and retired it in 2018 because hosts never opted in. The hold policy with expert opt-in moved from an open question to the strategic bet itself; see `02-competitive-intel/battlecard-and-bet.md`.

## 5. Success metrics

| Metric | Target | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| North star: seats per booking among segment organizers | At least 4 seats per group booking on average, read against a holdout of organizers who do not see the entry point. Derived from the segment: groups of 3 to 8 have a midpoint of 5.5, and 4 leaves room for the last friend dropping out. | This is the expand outcome. It shows a booking now carries several seats instead of one, and the holdout separates the feature's effect from organizers who would have booked anyway. |
| Leading indicator: group completion rate | The share of started group bookings that reach fully paid confirmation. Baseline is the current drop-off on same-slot group sessions; the target is set as a lift over that number once it is measured. Provisional bar: at least 80% of the individual booking completion rate, because a group has one more failure point than a solo booking, the last friend. | Completion shows whether the friend-side flow works, and it moves weeks before seats per booking does. |
| By-product: invited-friend signups and 90-day second booking | Measured, not targeted. | Shows whether the invite loop creates new Roamly users who stay, without making the launch depend on it. |
| Guardrails: held slots released unpaid; organizer time from invite to confirmation | Held slots released unpaid stay below the cost the supply side agreed to. Invite-to-confirmation stays in hours, not days. | The hold is the one new cost to hosts, and "minutes to value" has to hold for the whole group, not just the organizer. |

## 6. The assumption I'm most worried about

That the friends will complete. The organizer has the pain and gets the value. The friends are the majority of the transaction, have no pain today, and each one has to say yes, open a link and pay before the booking exists. If per-friend completion is 80%, a group of seven completes about 21% of the time (0.8 to the power of 7). If it is 95%, the same group completes about 70% of the time. The strategy depends on which of those two numbers is closer to the truth.

How to test it: put the one-screen guest-pay flow in front of 20 organizers in one launch city before the wider release, and measure per-friend completion and time to the last payment. If per-friend completion is below 90%, the friend flow is redesigned before the launch widens, and the hold window is lengthened so slots do not release while a group is still paying.
